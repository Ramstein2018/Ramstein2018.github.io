The flu, how to prevent it, treat it, and know when to worry.

Every winter the same questions arrive: Is this the flu or just a cold? Do I need the shot again? Is it too late for Tamiflu? Here's a physician's plain-English guide to influenza, what it actually is, how to tell it apart from the other bugs going around, and the warning signs that mean it's time to be seen.

People call almost any winter illness "the flu," but real influenza is a specific virus, and it hits differently. The good news: for most healthy adults it's a miserable week you ride out at home. The part that matters is knowing who is at genuine risk, when an antiviral actually helps, and which symptoms mean you should stop waiting and get seen.

What the flu actually is

Influenza is a respiratory virus, mainly influenza A and B, that spreads through droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks, and by touching a contaminated surface and then your face. It's contagious about a day before symptoms start, which is exactly why it moves through households, schools, and offices so efficiently: people spread it before they know they're sick.

Here's the common confusion worth clearing up: "flu" is not a catch-all for every winter bug. It isn't the "stomach flu" (that's usually norovirus, a completely different family), and it isn't the same as a head cold. True influenza has a signature, and recognizing that signature helps you make better decisions.

Flu vs. cold vs. COVID-19 vs. RSV

These four illnesses now circulate together every respiratory season, and they overlap enough that even physicians often can't tell them apart on symptoms alone. Still, the pattern gives useful clues.

  • Flu, comes on fast. People often describe being fine in the morning and flattened by afternoon: high fever, deep body aches, headache, and a bone-tired exhaustion that keeps you in bed. Cough and sore throat are common; a runny nose less so.
  • Common cold, comes on gradually and stays mild. Runny or stuffy nose, sneezing, mild sore throat, maybe a low-grade fever. You feel lousy but usually keep functioning.
  • COVID-19, the great imitator. It can mimic either a cold or the flu, and loss of smell or taste (less common with newer variants) still points toward it. Symptoms and timing vary widely.
  • RSV, cold-like in healthy adults, but a serious lower-airway infection in infants and frail older adults, where it can trigger wheezing, bronchiolitis, or pneumonia.

Let me be honest about the limits here: the pattern narrows the odds, but it doesn't confirm anything. A quick nasal swab in the office, often a combined flu/COVID test, is the only way to be sure, and that certainty matters when a positive result opens the door to an antiviral or changes who around you needs protecting.

The tell I trust most in clinic is the onset story. When someone tells me they were completely fine at breakfast and could barely get out of bed by dinner, fever, aching all over, wiped out, that abrupt crash is classic flu, and a cold almost never does that. But I still swab, because in a bad season I'll see flu, COVID, and RSV in the same afternoon, and the treatments aren't the same. Guessing is how the high-risk patient gets missed.

Who is actually at high risk

For most healthy people under 65, the flu is awful but self-limited. The reason we take it seriously is a specific set of groups in whom it turns dangerous, pneumonia, hospitalization, or worse. If you or someone you care for falls into one of these categories, the flu is not a "tough it out" illness:

  • Adults 65 and older, the group carrying most flu hospitalizations and deaths each year.
  • Young children, especially under 5 and particularly under 2.
  • Pregnancy, and the few weeks after delivery.
  • Chronic heart or lung disease, asthma, COPD, heart failure, coronary disease.
  • Diabetes and other metabolic conditions.
  • Immunocompromised patients, cancer treatment, transplant, HIV, long-term steroids or biologics.
  • People with kidney or liver disease, and residents of long-term care facilities.

For these patients, the strategy shifts from "rest and fluids" to "get evaluated early," because early treatment is where we prevent the bad outcomes.

The annual flu shot, and why "annual" is the whole point

The single most effective thing you can do is get vaccinated every fall. The question I hear most is why it isn't once-and-done like a childhood vaccine, and the answer is the virus itself: influenza drifts. Its surface proteins mutate constantly, so the strains circulating this winter aren't quite the ones from two winters ago. Each year, scientists forecast the likely strains and reformulate the vaccine to match. Last year's shot is aimed at last year's virus, which is why you need a fresh one.

Now the honest part about expectations. The flu shot is not a force field. In a season where the forecast matches well, it prevents a lot of illness; in a season where the match is imperfect, fewer people are fully protected. But here's what gets lost in the "the shot didn't work" grumbling: even a mismatched vaccine reliably reduces how sick you get. Vaccinated people who still catch the flu are far less likely to end up hospitalized or in the ICU. The shot's real job isn't only to keep you from ever getting sick, it's to keep the flu from becoming the dangerous version. Best timing is early fall (September–October), so protection is in place before the season peaks; but a later shot still beats none.

Antivirals: where Tamiflu helps, and where it doesn't

Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) is the antiviral most people have heard of, and it's genuinely useful, but only if the timing and the patient are right. It works by slowing the virus's ability to replicate, which means it's most effective started within about 48 hours of symptoms. Begin it early and it can shorten the illness by roughly a day and lower the odds of complications; start it late in an otherwise-healthy person and the benefit largely evaporates.

So the calculus is really about who: for a high-risk patient, I'll often start an antiviral quickly, sometimes even before the swab result is back, because the downside of waiting is a hospitalization I could have prevented. For a healthy 30-year-old who's already three days in, rest and fluids usually make more sense than a prescription. One thing an antiviral is not: a substitute for the vaccine. It treats an infection you already have; the shot is what keeps you from getting there.

Home care when you're otherwise healthy

If you're not in a high-risk group, the flu is mostly managed at home, and the fundamentals are unglamorous but effective: rest, fluids, and fever control. Sleep as much as your body demands. Drink steadily, water, broth, electrolyte drinks, because fever and poor appetite quietly dehydrate you. Acetaminophen or ibuprofen brings down fever and eases the body aches. Stay home until you've been fever-free for 24 hours without medication; you're protecting everyone you'd otherwise expose.

Warning signs, when to stop waiting

This is the part I most want people to keep. Call your doctor or go to the emergency room if you or someone you're caring for develops any of these:

  • Trouble breathing or shortness of breath.
  • Chest pain or pressure.
  • Confusion, unusual sleepiness, or difficulty waking.
  • A high fever that won't come down, or a fever lasting more than a few days.
  • Signs of dehydration, dizziness, little or no urination, no tears in a child.
  • Getting sick, improving, then getting worse again, often the sign of a secondary infection like pneumonia.
  • In children: fast or labored breathing, bluish lips, ribs pulling in with each breath, or not drinking fluids.

That "better, then worse again" pattern deserves special attention, it's the one people most often shrug off, and it's frequently the flu handing off to a bacterial pneumonia that needs treatment.

If there's one message I'd put on the refrigerator, it's this: the flu itself rarely kills a healthy person, its complications do. Trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion, or a rebound fever after you'd started feeling better are not "wait and see" symptoms. In an at-risk patient especially, that's the moment to call or come in, not the moment to power through another day.

Prevention beyond the shot

The vaccine does the heavy lifting, but simple habits add real protection during the season: wash your hands well and often, keep your hands away from your face, cover coughs, and, the most underrated one, stay home when you're sick. The most reliable way to keep the flu out of your household or workplace is for the person who has it not to bring it in.
